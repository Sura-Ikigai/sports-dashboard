Sports Dashboard · written for a first read, 2026-09-06

How the prediction model works

A walk through everything between “ESPN publishes a box score” and “the page says Detroit has a 56.8% chance”. No prior machine-learning knowledge assumed — the only maths below is multiplication, addition, and one curve, all of it worked through on a real game.

Companion to MODELING-V2-RESULT.md, which reports what the model scored. This one explains what it is. Every number here comes from the frozen model c97a9b14ddd5, the one that will run the 2026-27 season.

00

The one-sentence version

The model looks at four things that are knowable before a game starts, weighs each one, adds them up, and turns the total into a percentage. That is genuinely all it does. It is closer to a well-audited spreadsheet than to anything you would call artificial intelligence, and that is a deliberate choice rather than a limitation we settled for — every number it produces can be taken apart and explained, which is the point of the whole product.

What makes it hard is not the arithmetic. It is making sure the four numbers going in were computed from information that actually existed before tip-off, and that the score it gets on a season was not quietly achieved by peeking at that season first.

01

The data flow, end to end

Six stages. Each hands the next a narrower, more trustworthy thing. The module names are real — you can open any of them.

01
Fetch model/loader.py

Downloads each season’s schedule and box scores from the upstream ESPN feed. Before anything is parsed, the raw bytes are hashed with SHA-256 and compared against a hash pinned in the code. If a byte changed, the download is refused — not warned about, refused.

Out: raw season files, byte-for-byte the ones we recorded.

02
Clean and curate model/corpus.py

The feed mixes in games that are not games. All-Star exhibitions sit in the regular-season file under invented team ids. They are found by how few games those ids play rather than by a hardcoded list — next year’s All-Star game will carry fresh invented ids, and a hardcoded list would let them straight through. Two checks then have to pass: every season must retain exactly 30 franchises, and must shed exactly the number of exhibitions we pinned for it.

Out: 11,854 real games across 9 seasons.

03
Store model/ingest.py → Postgres → model/store.py

Verified rows land in the database. Migrations own the table shapes; the ingest command owns the data — no migration ever inserts a game. On the way out, the curation checks run again, so a row edited directly in the database is caught at read time rather than trusted because it once passed.

Out: corpus_games, corpus_player_box, corpus_team_box, corpus_venues.

04
Turn history into four numbers model/features.py · elo.py · availability.py

For any game, compute the four features from games that finished strictly before tip-off. This is the single most important boundary in the project, and §3 explains why.

Out: [elo_diff, home_b2b, away_b2b, avail_diff] for each game.

05
Fit and check model/splits.py · estimator.py · evaluate.py

Split the seasons in time, fit the weights on the earlier ones, score on a later one it has never seen. The fitted model is saved as plain JSON named by a hash of its own contents — so an edited model file is refused when loaded, and the same data always produces the same name.

Out: models/logistic-2026.json → version c97a9b14ddd5.

06
Serve model/schedule.py · job.py · routers/predictions.py

Once a day: refresh the live schedule, score every game tipping off in the next seven days, and append a prediction row for each. Never update one — see §6. The API reads those rows; the browser renders them.

Out: rows in predictions, and the pages you clicked through.

The warm-up seasons exist for one reason. Elo ratings start every team at the same number and take a while to sort themselves out. If 2022 were the first season the ratings ever saw, the model would spend its first training season learning from noise. So 2016–2019 are replayed to build the ratings up, and then their games are thrown away — they never appear as a row the model trains on or is scored against. A test enforces that.

2020 and 2021 are excluded on purpose. Games played in empty arenas broke home-court advantage: seasons before 2020 average 59.3% home wins, 2021 onward 55.2%. That is a real, structural change, not noise — and the two seasons in the middle belong to neither world. Including them would teach the model an average of two different sports.

Why this matters more than it sounds

The widely quoted “home teams win 58% of the time” is an artifact of averaging across that break. This project measures its own baseline instead — .55534, the home-win rate of the exact games it evaluates on — and every claim of improvement is made against that.

03

The four features

A “feature” is just a number describing the game before it is played. There are four. Every one is a home minus away difference or a yes/no flag, so a positive value always means “this favours the home team”.

elo_diff How much stronger the home team is

Every team carries a rating. Beat someone, it goes up; the size of the move depends on the margin and on who you beat — beating a strong team by 20 moves it more than beating a weak one by 2. Ratings carry across seasons at 75% strength, so a good team starts the next year good but not untouchably so. This feature is the home team’s rating minus the away team’s.

+0.7124weight
−.1268AUC if removed
home_b2b Did the home team play last night?

1 if yes, 0 if no. A team on the second night of a back-to-back is tired, and it shows. Note the weight is negative: playing last night makes the home team less likely to win, which is the direction you would expect and a useful sanity check that nothing is wired backwards.

−0.1510weight
−.0057AUC if removed
away_b2b Did the away team play last night?

Same question, other team — and kept as a separate feature rather than folded into one “rest difference”. That is deliberate: the two weights came out at 0.1510 and 0.1334, so a tired home team is penalised more than a tired away team. One combined feature would have been forced to average those into a number wrong for both.

+0.1334weight
−.0029AUC if removed
avail_diff Who has more of their usual team available

We have no injury feed, so this is inferred: take each team’s last 15 games, work out who its regular rotation is, then check what share of that rotation actually played most recently. The difference between the two teams is the feature. It is a proxy for health, not a measurement of it — see §7.

+0.1279weight
−.0031AUC if removed

The honest headline

Removing elo_diff costs .1268 of ranking ability. Removing the next-best costs .0057. That is 43 times. This is a one-feature model with three useful companions, and the analysis document says so in those words rather than describing it as a four-feature model.

The rule that governs all four

A feature may only be computed from games that finished strictly before the moment we are predicting from. Break that and the model looks brilliant and is worthless — it is “predicting” a result it was quietly shown. The industry name for this is leakage, and it is the failure that ends projects like this.

It is prevented structurally, not by care. The type that carries a game’s final score is a different type from the one used to describe an upcoming game, so a score cannot reach the feature code by accident. On top of that, a test adds games dated after the prediction moment and asserts not a single feature value moves — paired with a control that adds an earlier game and asserts values do move, so the test cannot pass by doing nothing.

04

From four numbers to one percentage

Here is the entire model, run on a real game — opening night 2026, Boston at Detroit. Follow the four rows down and you have done everything the model does.

What each column is doing

  1. Value

    The raw feature. Detroit’s rating is 8.64 points above Boston’s; neither team played last night; Detroit has very slightly less of its rotation available.

  2. Typical and Spread → standardising

    An Elo gap runs to hundreds; home_b2b is 0 or 1. Compared raw, Elo would dominate simply because its numbers are bigger. So each feature is re-expressed as “how many typical spreads away from typical is this?” — subtract the typical value, divide by the spread. After that, all four are on one scale and their weights are directly comparable.

  3. × weight → contribution

    The standardised value times the weight the fitting found. This is the number the waterfall on the game page draws. Note the second row: home_b2b is 0, which is below typical (0.144), and the weight is negative — two negatives make the contribution positive. “Detroit did not play last night” is genuinely good news for Detroit.

  4. Starting point

    Where a game sits before any feature is read — home-court advantage and everything else constant across all games. It is positive, which is why an evenly matched game still favours the home team.

  5. Total → percentage

    The total, +0.2723, is in log-odds — a scale that runs from minus infinity to plus infinity with 0 meaning “even”. One curve converts it to a probability: 1 / (1 + e−0.2723) = 0.5677. 56.8%.

Why log-odds, and why it matters to you

Probabilities cannot be added — 40% plus 30% is not 70% of anything useful. Log-odds can. That is what lets the game page show a waterfall where every bar sums exactly to the total with nothing left over. It is not an approximation or an attribution method; it is the same arithmetic, shown. Keeping the model simple enough for that to be true was a deliberate trade — a fancier model would need a separate estimation technique to guess at what this gives away for free, and the explanation is the product.

05

How the weights were found, and how we know they are real

Fitting

Nobody chose +0.7124. The fitting procedure starts with all weights at zero and adjusts them until the probabilities it produces best match what actually happened across 5,283 training games — “best” meaning it is rewarded for being confident and right and punished hard for being confident and wrong. A small penalty on large weights keeps any one feature from running away on a lucky streak.

It is about forty lines of standard-library Python. Two reasons: the model file can then be plain JSON rather than a format whose loading step can execute code, and the arithmetic is checkable — it is verified against the standard scikit-learn implementation to a maximum coefficient difference of 2.1×10⁻⁸.

Testing on a season it has never seen

You cannot shuffle games at random and hold some back. Shuffling would let the model train on March and be tested on November of the same season — it would already know how those teams turned out. So the split is always forward in time: train on earlier seasons, test on a later one. Three of them, because a single season carries roughly ±2.6 accuracy points of luck, and a pass/fail decided on one season is substantially decided by chance.

The 2026 season was sealed. Nobody looked at it while choosing features or settings. Which features to keep was decided on 2024 and 2025 only, with the rule for keeping them written down and committed before it ran — a threshold chosen after seeing the result is not a threshold. Every time the sealed season is scored, the run is appended to a log file, so “we tried twenty things and reported the best” is not a story anyone can tell without it being visible.

Accuracy alone is gameable — a model can be 65% accurate with meaningless probabilities. So the bar was paired: it had to clear 62% accuracy and beat a predictor that just says “.55534, always”. Both were met.

On calibration the model errs the useful way: every confidence band above a coin flip wins more often than it claims. A “Lean” claims 59.9% and delivers 62.9%. Under-promising is the error you want, because a visitor who trusts the number is not misled.

06

What runs every day, once the season starts

The model is now frozen. Nothing about it changes during 2026-27 — that is what makes the season a real test rather than another backtest. What runs daily is the job:

  1. Refresh the schedule

    Re-fetch the live schedule file. It changes nightly — scores fill in, games get postponed, and the NBA Cup placeholder rows resolve into real matchups under the same ids. It cannot be pinned by hash the way finished seasons are, so it is checked for plausibility instead: known teams, sane statuses, and a game count that may grow but never shrink.

  2. Score the next seven days

    Compute the four features as of now, run the arithmetic, get a probability.

  3. Append — never update

    A prediction made six days out and one made an hour before tip-off are two different true statements, not one stale and one fresh: the rest situation genuinely differs. Both are kept. The accuracy record scores the last one before tip-off.

A nice detail

“Append, never update” is not a convention anyone has to remember — the database account the job uses has permission to insert rows and no permission to change or delete them. It could not overwrite its own history if it tried.

Not done yet

Nothing currently runs the job on a schedule. It has to be triggered by a cron or a supervised runner before 2026-10-20, with some way of noticing if it stops — because the failure is silent: the site keeps working and simply shows nothing new.

07

What to collect this season

Three of these already happen. The fourth is the one that matters, and it has a deadline.

Daily predictions automatic

Every prediction, keyed by game, model version and the moment it was made. This is the track record — without it there is nothing to judge the season by.

Schedule snapshots automatic

Every accepted fetch of the schedule, with its hash and counts. Answers “what did we know when we predicted this?” — the question a prediction actually raises.

Results automatic

Final scores arrive on the live schedule feed and feed straight back into the ratings, so a prediction made in February uses February’s Elo rather than last June’s.

Injury reports decide before 2026-10-20

This is the only item that cannot be collected later. Today’s availability feature is inferred from who recently played, because no historical archive of injury reports exists to train a real one on. That is a chicken-and-egg, and it can be broken in exactly one direction: start archiving now and the archive exists next cycle. By June, 2026-27’s reports are gone. The cost is a table and a nightly fetch; the alternative is arriving at the next cycle with the same excuse.

08

What would actually make it better

Worth saying first, because it shapes everything else: three cycles of re-encoding features have converged on roughly .73 AUC. Phase 1 got there with a point-differential model; this cycle rebuilt the feature set from scratch and landed in the same place on the sealed season. What it did buy was consistency — the season-to-season swing more than halved.

The read is that the ceiling is not in the arithmetic. It is in the information. The corpus knows who played and what the score was; it does not know who was hurt, who was resting, or who is in the starting five tonight.

IdeaWhat it needsWhy it might move the number
Real injury status The archive above, starting now Replaces a proxy with the thing itself. The single largest gap between what the model sees and what a person betting on the game would look at.
Starting-lineup continuity Nothing new — already ingested Distinguishes “everyone is healthy” from “the same five who have played together all month”. Cheap to try.
Rest beyond back-to-backs Nothing new Today the model knows “played last night or not”. Three days off and one day off are treated identically, and they are not.
Pace and efficiency Nothing new — already ingested Elo compresses a team into one number. Two teams with equal ratings can be very different matchups.
In-game state A live feed Would predict far better and answers a different question — this model exists to say something before tip-off. Listed for completeness, not proposed.

The lesson from this cycle

Cycle 2 added three new features and each is worth .003–.006 of AUC. More features are not obviously the answer. Whatever gets tried next should be pre-registered the same way — the rule for keeping it written down and committed before the measurement runs — or it becomes very easy to find something that works on the seasons you happened to look at.

One thing not to do

Swapping in a more powerful model class — a gradient-boosted tree, say — is the obvious suggestion and it is the wrong trade here. It would buy a fraction of a point and cost the exact decomposition the game page is built on. The contribution waterfall would become an estimate produced by a separate technique rather than the model’s own arithmetic shown honestly, and the explanation is the product.
